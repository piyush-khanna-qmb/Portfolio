How (and Why) Would a Robot Have a 'Vedic' Mind

Chitta, Manas, Ahankaram, Buddhi. It started with me trying to work out how my own mind worked, which turns out to be a strange thing to attempt with the mind in question.

Anushka is usually described in the press as India's first Vedic-principled humanoid robot. It is a good line and it has carried the project a long way, but it is also the kind of phrase that sounds like marketing. I want to explain what it actually means in code, because the engineering reason was real and came first.

First, what 'Vedic' actually means here

The Vedas are the oldest layer of Sanskrit scripture, and the philosophical tradition that grew out of them spent a very long time on one question: what is a mind made of. I use Vedic loosely here, the way it is usually used in conversation, to mean that Indic tradition of thinking about consciousness rather than a specific verse in a specific text. The particular model I borrowed, the antahkarana or inner instrument, is set out most clearly in the Vedantic and Samkhya material that came later.

So it is not scripture in a robot. It is a decomposition of mind, worked out over centuries by people who had nothing to study except their own attention, applied to a scheduling problem.

Where this actually started

This did not begin with Anushka. It began in my second year, before ChatGPT existed and before any of this was a conversation anyone was having casually. I was a curious student who wanted to build a system that could think, and I had no idea what that sentence even meant.

So I went backwards. I dug out the books I had read between seventh and twelfth standard on psychology, how the brain works, consciousness, and what actually drives a person to act. Not because I expected a blueprint, but because I had nowhere else to look.

The problem was never perception. I had been comfortable with computer vision and NLP since twelfth standard, and a system that sees or hears is a solved exercise. The problem was making those faculties coexist without tripping over each other.

  • •The hearing system has to mindfully ignore the robot's own voice. Otherwise she hears herself speak, treats it as input, and starts answering herself.
  • •The microcontrollers in the hand have no way of knowing whether the arm is rising to wave at someone she recognises or because a speech command told it to. Those are different intentions behind an identical movement.
  • •And the harder question underneath that one: does the arm need to know? Which layer is supposed to hold the intent, and which layers are allowed to stay ignorant?

That last question is the one that sent me looking for a model rather than writing more rules.

Hacking your own system

To design an artificial brain I first had to understand how a real one works, so I could copy it. And there is something genuinely strange about that task: it was my brain, trying to account for everything it does and why it does it.

It is like trying to hack a system while running inside it, using the only instrument you are trying to reverse engineer.

You cannot step outside it to get a clean look. Every observation you make about your own attention is itself an act of attention. That is the point at which I stopped trying to derive the thing from scratch and went looking for people who had already spent centuries on exactly this, with exactly the same handicap.

The problem with the standard loop

Most robots run a flat pipeline. Sense, process, decide, act. It works, and for a robot arm in a cell it is entirely sufficient.

It falls apart for a receptionist. Anushka stands in a college lobby. At any moment there are six conversations in earshot, people walking past who have no interest in her, someone addressing her directly, and someone she spoke to yesterday walking back in. A flat loop has no principled way to decide which of those deserves a response. Everything arrives as input with equal standing, and you end up writing an escalating pile of special-case rules.

What was needed was not better perception. It was a layered model of attention, something that could hold 'I perceived this', 'this concerns me', and 'I should act on this' as genuinely different states.

Four terms that turned out to be an architecture

The Vedic model of the inner instrument, the antahkarana, splits mind into four functions rather than treating it as one thing. I researched the structure and found that it mapped onto my problem with uncomfortable precision.

  • •Manas: the sensory mind. Takes in, registers, and crucially does not decide. It is the faculty of doubt and oscillation.
  • •Chitta: the storehouse. Impressions, memory, accumulated residue of past encounters.
  • •Ahankara: the I-maker. The function that determines self-reference: is this mine, is this about me.
  • •Buddhi: discriminative intellect. The faculty that actually resolves and commits to a course.

Read that list as a systems engineer rather than a philosopher and it stops being metaphysics. It is a four-stage attention pipeline with a memory store and an explicit relevance filter, which is exactly the thing I needed and had failed to design on my own.

How it maps in the system

Manas is the intake layer. Speech recognition across forty-odd languages, face detection, object tracking, gesture input. It registers everything and resolves nothing. It is permitted to be uncertain and to hold competing readings simultaneously.

Chitta is persistent memory. Faces she has encountered, what was discussed, what someone asked for last time. Not a cache, but a store of impressions that shapes how later input is interpreted.

Ahankara is the piece that does the most work and gets the least attention. It answers one question about every incoming stimulus: is this addressed to me? Someone saying 'where is the library' two metres away, facing elsewhere, mid-conversation with a friend, is not talking to Anushka. A flat architecture has to be told that with a rule. A system with an explicit self-reference layer treats it as the normal operation of a dedicated faculty.

Most of being a good receptionist is correctly determining what is not your business.

Buddhi is the decision layer, and it sits on top of BOSS, the Bot Operations Scheduling System I wrote to run her. It takes what Manas registered, what Chitta knows, and what Ahankara flagged as self-relevant, and commits to one action. It is the only layer permitted to commit. That restriction is the whole point.

Why not just build it directly

A fair objection: all of this could have been designed from first principles, with ordinary names, as an attention pipeline. The four Sanskrit terms add nothing a well-drawn block diagram would not.

Possibly. But I did not design it from first principles. I tried, and what I produced was a flat loop with rules bolted on, because that is what you naturally produce when you are deep in the problem. The model gave me a decomposition that was already worked out, already load-bearing, and (this is the part I did not expect) already stress-tested against exactly my failure mode. The distinction between registering and committing is not an obvious design insight. It is the first thing this model insists on.

Borrowing a structure that has been refined for three thousand years is not mysticism. It is the same instinct as reaching for a known algorithm instead of inventing one at three in the morning.
