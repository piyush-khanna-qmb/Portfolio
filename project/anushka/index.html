Robotics · India's Most Advanced Humanoid Robot

Anushka

A humanoid robot that recognises faces, objects and gestures, and understands and responds to speech in multiple languages. Took four years to actually build, but the real story starts a lot earlier than that, in a 7th-grade bedroom, with a script that could only ever say what I'd already told it to.

A promise on the last day of school

It all started with an introvert boy, his laptop, and the excitement to see it speak. I remember it was the end of 7th standard, a lazy Sunday morning in a retreating winter, when I stumbled onto SAPI, the speech engine built into my Windows 8 laptop, and heard it say back, “Good morning sir.”
That one line was enough to send me down a rabbit hole. I started writing Visual Basic scripts around that same engine, and what came out of it, an assistant I named Ganpat, could only answer prompts I'd already written for it myself, nothing soft about it at all. On the last day of 12th standard, I told my classmates I'd eventually build two full-scale robots: Anushka, and a second one called Vinayak.

A box of loose wires

The next real step came during the COVID lockdown, in my first year of college. My sister, who studied ECE, introduced me to Arduino and microcontrollers. I ordered a kit along with a few sensors and relay modules and used them to wire up my first home automation setup by the end of that year, controlled over an HC-05 Bluetooth module through an Android app I wrote in Java. The whole thing was flaky. The wires were crammed loosely into a box and it kept short-circuiting on me.

Outgrowing Bluetooth

Bluetooth's range was the obvious ceiling, so I did the obvious thing and complained about it to a professor at the start of second year. He pointed me at the ESP32, which had only just come out. But wanting to control the whole system from anywhere, not just from inside Bluetooth's modest little bubble, meant actually learning web development instead of improvising my way through it. By the end of second year I'd taught myself the full MERN stack, mostly out of necessity.

Ganpat learns to understand sentences

By fall 2022 I'd rebuilt Ganpat with a machine learning layer underneath him, so he could actually parse natural language instead of just matching the exact phrases I'd hardcoded back in school. That one upgrade is what let me manage tasks, play music, set schedules, run the whole home automation setup from anywhere in the world over Wi-Fi. And this is the part people usually miss: it's the same thread that eventually became the decision-making model inside Anushka. Not a side project that happened to come later. The same one, just aimed at something much harder.

Toy cars wearing the word 'robot'

College had only just reopened after the lockdown year, and I went straight to the robotics lab expecting more than I found there: toy cars, RC cars, line-followers, all filed under 'robots.' What did catch my attention was a set of actuator arms. I remember picturing them immediately: taken apart, rebuilt longer with heavier motors, mounted on a mannequin as the start of something with an actual presence. It worked. The first time it moved, the lab erupted. It wasn't just students crowding around either, teachers, HoDs and even the helping staff came by to see the torso move.

The three of us, most days

Once that worked, the project needed more hands than mine. Harsh took on the mechanical structure, the base, and mounting the rebuilt arms onto the mannequin. Atul Sir, who ran the lab we set up Anushka's torso in, handled the electrical side.
Phase 1 ran from around October 2022 through to a functioning prototype by January 2023. Phase 2 followed: a new base module, cut and built once university exams ended in March 2023.

The hardest summer

Phase 3 started right after my birthday that year. Summer arrived and brought the heaviest stretch of the whole build with it. We all fell sick around the same time the seasons changed, and kept working anyway, eleven in the morning to seven the next. The last stretch was a three-day no-sleep push to get the whole assembly working together, and it did. That's when Anushka actually became a face, and when the Palangtodh Sprint, the sprint format my team kept running well after Anushka was done, got its name.

Showing her to the world

Phase 3 ended with a demonstration at IIT Bombay on the first of August, 2023. Later, people I never expected a student project to meet came to see her in person: Atul Kothari, General Secretary of the RSS, and Uttar Pradesh's Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath, who formally inaugurated her, both visiting the lab she was built in.

A heartbeat that wasn't supposed to matter

She recognises faces, objects and gestures, and understands and responds to speech across multiple languages. One detail travelled further than I expected in the coverage: A Mechanically Simulated Heartbeat!
It was a small addition, but it was the thing that made people stop and look rather than just photograph her and move on. Underneath all of it, the jaw, the neck, the eyes, the hands each ran on their own microcontroller in their own little world, so I wrote an operating system from scratch to get them reporting to one master computer without stepping on each other: BOSS, Bot Operations Scheduling System, built on WSL.

A mind borrowed from the Vedas

Her decision-making runs on the antahkarana, the four-part “inner instrument” set out in Vedantic and Samkhya philosophy, rather than a flat sense-process-decide-act loop. I started there because it was the clearest account I could find of how attention, memory and intent coexist without stepping on each other, and it happened to be written down in Sanskrit philosophy rather than a systems textbook.
